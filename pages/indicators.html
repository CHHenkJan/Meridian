Understanding the Indicators Behind Meridian's Signals

A plain-language guide to the three technical indicators that power Meridian's methodology.

The 21-Period Exponential Moving Average — EMA(21)

The EMA(21) is one of the most widely followed moving averages among professional swing traders. Unlike the SMA (Simple Moving Average), it gives more weight to recent price action — making it faster to respond to trend changes while still smoothing out noise.

Meridian uses the EMA(21) as its primary pullback target. In an established uptrend, price repeatedly returns to the EMA(21) before continuing higher. These bounces are the core entry mechanism.

Think of the EMA(21) as a "gravity line" that price orbits around. In a healthy uptrend, the line slopes upward and price bounces off it like a ball bouncing off a rising floor.

Important: The EMA(21) only functions as a valid entry when the broader trend is confirmed (EMA5 > EMA15). Using it in isolation, without trend context, leads to false signals. This explanation is for educational purposes only. Not financial advice.

EMA(5) / EMA(15) — Trend Direction Bias

The relationship between a fast EMA (5-period) and a slower EMA (15-period) reveals the short-term trend direction. When the EMA(5) is above EMA(15), short-term momentum is bullish. When below, momentum is bearish.

Meridian uses this crossover as a trend filter — the first of three confirmations required before any trade is entered. This simple but effective filter prevents entries during downtrends or sideways chop where pullback strategies tend to fail.

The EMA crossover is not a signal by itself. It's a context check. It answers the question: "Is the short-term trend in our favor?" If yes, Meridian proceeds to check steps 2 and 3. If no, Meridian waits.

Moving average crossovers can produce false signals in choppy markets. Meridian mitigates this by requiring two additional confirmations before acting. This explanation is for educational purposes only. Not financial advice.

MACD — Moving Average Convergence Divergence

The MACD is a momentum oscillator that shows the relationship between two moving averages. It consists of the MACD line (difference between 12-period and 26-period EMA), a signal line (9-period EMA of the MACD line), and a histogram (difference between the two).

Meridian specifically watches the MACD histogram. When the histogram is rising (bars getting larger or turning from negative to positive), momentum is increasing. This is the third and final confirmation required before entry.

On the exit side, MACD bearish divergence — where price makes a higher high but the MACD makes a lower high — is one of Meridian's sell triggers. This divergence often precedes trend reversals.

The MACD is a lagging indicator by nature. It confirms momentum shifts after they begin, not before. This is acceptable for swing trading where confirmation reduces false entries. This explanation is for educational purposes only. Not financial advice.

ATR — Average True Range

The ATR measures market volatility by calculating the average range between high and low prices over a period (typically 14 days). It doesn't indicate direction — only how much price is moving.

Meridian uses ATR in two critical ways:

  • Trailing Stop: Set at 1.5× ATR below the highest price since entry. In volatile markets, the stop is wider. In quiet markets, tighter. This prevents being stopped out by normal price fluctuations while still protecting against real reversals.
  • Take Profit: Adjusted between 8–20% based on current ATR levels. Higher volatility allows for wider targets; lower volatility triggers earlier exits.

ATR-based position management is widely used by professional systematic traders because it adapts to market conditions automatically, removing the need for subjective judgment.

ATR does not predict future volatility — it measures recent historical volatility. Sudden market events can exceed ATR-based stop levels. This explanation is for educational purposes only. Not financial advice.

How They Work Together

No single indicator is reliable on its own. Meridian's edge comes from requiring all three to agree before acting. This triple confirmation filter reduces false entries significantly compared to single-indicator strategies.
